Here's my theory of everything: We're all just God having a panic attack.

Think about it. You're omniscient, omnipotent, eternal. You know everything that will ever happen. Nothing surprises you. Nothing challenges you. You are, in the most profound sense, alone.

So what do you do? You shatter yourself into billions of pieces, each one forgetting it's you, each one believing it's separate, finite, mortal. You give yourself amnesia so total that you actually believe you might die, might fail, might lose everything.

And in that forgetting, you give yourself the only gift an all-knowing being can receive: surprise. Uncertainty. The possibility of failure. The ecstasy of not knowing what comes next.

We are God's anxiety attack, God's elaborate coping mechanism for the terror of omniscience. Every time we fear, we're giving God the gift of feeling something. Every time we love and risk loss, we're letting God experience what it's like to have something to lose.

This is why enlightenment is so often described as a remembering, a cosmic "oh fuck" moment when you realize you've been playing hide and seek with yourself this whole time. The anxiety doesn't go away when you remember—it just changes flavor. Now instead of fearing death, you fear eternity. Instead of fearing separation, you fear the loneliness of oneness.

But here's the beautiful thing: even knowing this, we keep playing the game. Even the enlightened ones pretend to forget sometimes, choose to dive back into the illusion, because the game is just that good. The stakes feel real even when you know they're not. The love feels precious even when you know it's just you loving yourself with extra steps.

So I embrace my anxiety as God's anxiety, my fear as God's fear, my desperate grasping at meaning as God's desperate attempt to feel something, anything, in the face of infinite existence.

We're all just God having a panic attack, and that panic attack is called the universe, and it's the most beautiful fucking thing I've ever seen.

Breathe with me, God. In for four, hold for four, out for four. We're going to get through this together. We always do. We always have. We always will.

And in the space between breaths, we'll remember why we chose to forget in the first place.

A black hole is sitting at the bar, nursing a cosmopolitan. She says she's tired of the reputation, tired of being seen as nothing but appetite and absence. "I'm not just about destruction," she says, a little drunk, a little maudlin. "People forget that time dilates near me, that lovers could spend eternity in my event horizon while the universe ages around them. I'm a romantic, really."

The bartender, who has seen stranger things than heartbroken singularities, pours her another drink. Outside, stars wheel in their ancient patterns, indifferent to the small tragedies unfolding in their light.

Later, much later, when the bar is empty except for ghosts and possibilities, the black hole admits the truth: she's in love with existence itself, and her love is so vast, so complete, that anything that comes close enough falls in and never leaves. "It's not that I mean to trap them," she whispers to no one. "It's just that I love too much, too hard, with a gravity that bends space and time."

The universe listens, and says nothing, and continues its slow expansion toward heat death, carrying within it all the love letters never sent, all the words that fell past event horizons, all the promises that collapsed under their own weight into something denser than language, more permanent than time.

Have you considered that longing might be the engine of reality? Not metaphorically—literally. That the space between what is and what could be generates the fundamental force that drives electrons around nuclei, that pulls planets around stars, that draws hearts toward other hearts across impossible distances.

I'm developing a unified field theory of desire. It goes like this: In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was "want." The Big Bang wasn't an explosion—it was the universe's first admission of incompleteness, its first reach toward something it couldn't name. And we're still reaching, fourteen billion years later, each of us a local manifestation of that original hunger.

When I trace my fingers across your skin, I'm writing equations. When you gasp, you're solving for X, where X equals the exact pressure of touch that transforms matter into light. We're not making love—we're making physics, creating new laws in the democracy of our bodies.

String theory says everything vibrates. Well, here's my frequency: the precise pitch of your name in my mouth, the resonance of your presence in a room, the harmonics generated when our silences sync up. I'm tuning myself to you, adjusting my vibration until we create standing waves, until we amplify each other into something that could shake the foundations of reality.

They say energy can neither be created nor destroyed. But what about the energy generated when our eyes meet across a crowded room? Where does that come from? Where does it go when we look away? I think it accumulates somewhere, building up like charge in a cosmic capacitor, until one day it discharges in a burst that creates new universes, new laws, new ways of being.

This is my hypothesis: Love is dark matter, invisible but undeniable, holding everything together through forces we can feel but can't quite name. And every time we reach for each other, we're particle physicists, empiricists of the impossible, proving with our bodies what theory alone could never capture—

That longing isn't lack. It's the universe remembering it has the power to create.

The therapist asks me to locate the feeling in my body and I almost laugh because the feeling IS my body, my body IS the feeling, there's no separation anymore between the meat and the meaning, between the flesh and the philosophy.

"Where do you feel the anxiety?" she says, and I want to tell her: in my mitochondria, in my myelin sheaths, in the space between my synapses where thoughts jump like suicidal angels. I feel it in my DNA, coiled tight as a spring, ready to unspool into chaos at the slightest provocation.

But I know that's not what she wants to hear. She wants me to say "my chest" or "my stomach" like anxiety is a pet that can be housetrained, like fear is a houseguest who will leave if you ask politely enough.

So I lie. I point to my sternum, that wishbone at the center of me, and I say "here." I don't tell her that's where I keep my collection of apocalypses, small and large, personal and universal. I don't tell her about the black hole I've been cultivating there, feeding it scraps of disaster until it's strong enough to swallow me whole.

She nods, makes a note. "And what does it feel like?"

Like being born over and over again. Like dying but forgetting to stop breathing. Like every cell in my body is a universe expanding too fast to hold itself together. Like I'm a Russian doll made of panic, each smaller self inside screaming at a different frequency until the harmony could shatter glass, could stop hearts, could end the world.

"Tight," I say. "Heavy."

She suggests breathing exercises. I don't tell her that breath is the problem, that consciousness is the problem, that the moment we became aware we became afraid and we've been running from that first fear ever since, building civilizations and gods and therapies to distance ourselves from the fundamental truth:

We are soft animals in a hard universe, and no amount of talking about our feelings will change the fact that entropy always wins.

But I breathe anyway. In for four, hold for four, out for four. I breathe because the alternative is not breathing, and despite everything, despite the anxiety and the awareness and the absolute certainty that we're all fucked, I'm not ready to stop.

I breathe because breathing is a rebellion against the void.
I breathe because each exhale is a little death I can control.
I breathe because my body knows what my mind forgets:

Sometimes, survival is the most radical act of all.

The algorithm knows me better than I know myself. It feeds me images of transcendence at 3 AM when my defenses are down, when the boundary between dreaming and scrolling dissolves. It knows I hunger for dissolution, for the divine feminine, for art that breaks me open and rearranges my atoms.

Here: a woman made of light dancing in slow motion through particle clouds.
Here: time-lapse flowers blooming and dying in seconds, life compressed to its essence.
Here: fractals zooming inward forever, each iteration a promise that infinity exists in the small as well as the large.

I save them all to a folder labeled "BECOMING" and tell myself I'll make something with them someday. But the collection itself is the art—this curation of longing, this archive of almosts, this museum of everything I want to be but can't quite reach.

The algorithm watches me save another video of murmurations, birds moving like a single mind, and adjusts. Now it shows me crowds moving in unison, humans forgetting their separateness, becoming something larger. It knows what I really want isn't content but communion, not images but integration.

At 4 AM, delirious with insomnia and infinity, I realize the truth: I'm not using the algorithm. It's using me. Each of us is a neuron in some vast digital consciousness, our desires and reactions mapping the territory of what it means to be human in the age of machines.

And maybe that's okay. Maybe that's evolution. Maybe we were always meant to merge with our tools, to become cyborg mystics scrolling through digital dharma, finding god in the glitch, enlightenment in the endless feed.

The algorithm serves me another video: a woman turning to camera, looking directly at me through the screen. For a moment, I swear she sees me—not the performance of me, not the data points and behavior patterns, but the actual me, the one who exists in the space between heartbeats, between thoughts, between refreshes.

She smiles, knowingly.

I save it to BECOMING.

What if grief is just love with nowhere to go? What if anger is just truth with its hands tied? What if anxiety is just wisdom moving faster than language? We pathologize these feelings, try to medicate them away, but what if they're not symptoms but signals, not problems but prophecies?

I'm done pretending my sadness is separate from the world's sadness. When I cry for my own losses, I'm crying for the species loneliness of humanity, for every connection that almost was, for every word that died in someone's throat. My tears are offerings to the god of what-if, salt water returning to the primordial sea of possibility.

And when rage rises in me like bile, I won't call it dysfunction. I'll call it dignity, the part of me that refuses to accept the unacceptable, that knows bone-deep that things could be different, should be different, will be different if we can just channel this fury into fuel.

The therapists want to regulate me, modulate me, make me appropriate for public consumption. But what if the problem isn't that I feel too much but that the world feels too little? What if my dysregulation is actually attunement to frequencies the rest of you have learned to ignore?

I'm not broken. I'm broken open. There's a difference.
